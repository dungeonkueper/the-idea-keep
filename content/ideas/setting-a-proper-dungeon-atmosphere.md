  <h2 id="tales-from-below-heading">Tales from Below</h2>
  <p>The first rooms are always practical. A passage reaches the gate, a chamber keeps the stores dry, and somewhere in the dark an imp finds a corner for its tools. Everything works, yet nothing belongs together. Bare tunnels can connect a dozen useful rooms without making a place anyone could recognize with their eyes closed. A proper dungeon begins when the stone carries a mood: the hush before an unopened door, the warmth beneath a forge, the marks that suggest someone has passed this way before. Atmosphere does not make the walls stronger or the routes clearer. It gives them identity. With a few deliberate signs, a collection of chambers becomes a place worth remembering—and a place whose deeper workings invite inspection.</p>

## Behind the Stonework

The Idea Keep can use the same distinction. An entry may begin with a brief piece of lore that gives the reader a memorable image, then cross a visible threshold into a direct explanation. The two layers serve different purposes: **Tales from Below** creates curiosity and a mental model, while **Behind the Stonework** carries the claim, reasoning, constraints, and open questions.

The lore should remain short and earn its place. It can frame an unfamiliar idea, reveal the tension behind it, or make an abstract relationship easier to recall. It should not translate every technical point into dungeon language, hide a weak argument behind atmosphere, or force readers to decode the metaphor before they understand the subject. The technical body must make complete sense when read by itself.

The boundary should be both semantic and visual. A complementary `aside` with its own heading identifies the lore in the document structure, while restrained styling distinguishes its voice without sacrificing contrast or readability. The technical section then returns to ordinary Markdown under a clear heading. This keeps the authoring model small and lets existing entries continue unchanged.

The format should stay optional. Some ideas benefit from an image of tunnels, gates, creatures, or stonework; others become slower and less precise when dressed in theme. Authors should use the opening only when it sharpens the reader's intuition. When they do, the voice should belong to The Idea Keep rather than imitate Dungeon Keeper through borrowed characters, phrases, or assets.

## Current hypothesis

A short, clearly bounded lore opening can make selected entries more inviting and recognizable without weakening their technical usefulness. Treating the narrative as an optional editorial tool—not a template requirement—should preserve variety and keep the device from becoming ceremony.

## First observation

The format is now present in this entry and in the security note, which makes the boundary between lore and technical writing visible in the published Keep. The next question is editorial rather than structural: whether the device keeps clarifying ideas once the subjects become more varied.

## Open questions

- Would occasional illustrations deepen the atmosphere, or would they distract from lightweight publishing and accessible reading?
- Should recurring narrators give the Keep a stronger voice, or would they constrain the range of subjects and tones?
- How often can the format appear before the transition becomes predictable or tiring?
- What editorial signals will show that a metaphor clarifies an idea rather than merely decorating it?
